CAREERS & MAJORS · 2026 DATA

The highest-paying major right now is also one of the hardest to get a job with. Here's how both can be true.

> **QUICK ANSWER**
>
> Computer science graduates get the highest starting pay of any major (about $81,500). At the same time, they have one of the highest unemployment rates of any major right out of college (about 7%).
>
> Both of these are true at once. Picking a major by looking at just one number — pay OR job odds — misses half the picture. Look at both below before you decide.

## Column 1: what different majors pay

The Federal Reserve Bank of New York tracks pay for graduates ages 22–27, across over 70 majors. In their 2026 numbers, engineering majors fill 8 of the top 10 highest-paying spots.

| Right after college (age 22–27) | Median pay | Years into career (age 35–45) | Median pay |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Computer engineering (highest) | $90,000 | Chemical / computer / aerospace engineering (top group) | $115,000–$135,000 |
| Computer science, chemical & aerospace engineering | $80,000+ | Economics & finance | About $110,000 |
| A typical major in the middle of the list | $45,000–$60,000 | Majors reaching six figures | 21 out of 73 |
| Foreign languages (lowest) | $40,000 | Early childhood education (lowest) | $49,000 |

## Column 2: who's actually getting hired

Here's where the story flips. As of March 2026, about 5.6% of all recent college graduates are unemployed — that's higher than the 4.2% unemployment rate for adults overall. But the majors struggling most aren't the ones most families expect:

| Major | Unemployed right after college | What this means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Anthropology | 7.9% | The highest of any major |
| Computer engineering | 7.8% | 2nd highest — even though it also pays the most |
| Computer science | About 7% | 5th highest, despite top-tier pay |
| All recent graduates | 5.6% | The average to compare everything else against |
| Philosophy / art history | About 3% | Roughly half the computer science rate |
| Nutrition sciences | 0.4% | The lowest of any major tracked |

## How can pay be high but jobs be hard to find?

The problem is hitting new grads hardest, not the whole field — there have been over 500,000 tech layoffs recently, AI is now doing a lot of the basic coding work junior employees used to do, and there are more computer science graduates than ever competing for the same entry-level jobs. Experienced developers aren't struggling nearly as much — it's mainly people just starting out who are feeling this.

The long-term outlook still looks strong — the U.S. government predicts software jobs will grow about 15% between 2024 and 2034. That's about 5 times faster than the average job. Unemployment right now is a snapshot of this year. A job growth prediction is about the years after that.

This isn't just a computer science problem — more than 40% of recent grads across all majors are working jobs that don't actually require a college degree. No major fully protects you from a tough job market right now.

## How to actually use this info

- Don't look at just one number — computer science has high pay AND a harder time getting hired right now. That doesn't mean don't pick it — it means plan for a tougher first job search: get internships, build a specific skill (like cybersecurity or AI), and have real projects to show, not just a degree.
- An internship is worth more than switching majors — students with internship experience earn about 10–15% more in their first job, and have a much better shot at getting an offer before they even graduate. That's a bigger, more useful lever than most of the pay differences between similar majors.
- These numbers are averages, not guarantees — someone who works hard in a lower-paying major can absolutely out-earn someone coasting through a higher-paying one. But averages are still the most honest starting point when you're choosing — and the averages above can differ by 2x depending on the major.
