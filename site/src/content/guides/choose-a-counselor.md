> **The short answer:** hire a college counselor the way you would hire any professional for a high-stakes job. Get every fee and the refund policy in writing, confirm their credentials in an association directory yourself, and walk away from anyone who guarantees results or offers to write your student's essays. The 10 checks below come from the ethics codes of the three main professional associations (NACAC, IECA and HECA) and from the integrity rules UC and the Common App apply to every applicant. For each check, we tell you what to ask, what a good answer sounds like, and how to verify it without taking anyone's word for it.

## The 10 checks at a glance

1. Every fee and the refund policy are in writing before work starts.
2. No guarantees: not of admission, specific colleges, or aid amounts.
3. Your student writes every word of every essay.
4. The counselor takes no money from colleges for placing your student.
5. Their association membership shows up in the association's own directory.
6. They know this year's rules for the colleges your student is applying to.
7. You know who does the work, how many students they carry, and what they refer out.
8. They plan for cost and financial aid, not just admission.
9. They protect your student's data and application status.
10. They report results with denominators, not logo walls.

## Why choosing a counselor is harder than it looks

Anyone can charge families for college advice. The head of the Independent Educational Consultants Association has said that not a single U.S. state licenses educational consultants, so there is no license number to look up.

Comparison shopping is also hard by design. In a 2025 survey of 600 independent counselors, 83% did not publish their rates, and 48% said three-quarters or more of their business came from past-client referrals. Most families end up choosing between the one or two names a neighbor mentioned.

This guide replaces word of mouth with checks you can run yourself. Each one traces to a public standard: the NACAC Guide to Ethical Practice in College Admission (2026 edition), the IECA Principles of Good Practice (December 2024), the HECA Standards and Ethics Statement, and UC and Common App integrity rules.

A note on our stake: D2Excel is an open marketplace for independent college counselors, so we benefit when families hire one. That is exactly why every check below points to a standard you can read yourself, and why we include the cases where you don't need a paid counselor at all.

## School counselor, independent counselor, or firm: what each actually gives you

The real difference between your options is hours per student, not credentials. A public high school counselor has roughly 1.5 hours a year for each student's college planning; a typical independent counselor package runs 21 to 40 hours.

| Option | Typical cost to your family | College-planning time per student | Best fit when |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Public high school counselor | Free | About 1.5 hours a year (our estimate, below); about 6 if all of it went to seniors | Your school has a dedicated college counselor, and your student's list is mostly UC and CSU |
| Independent counselor (solo practice) | $5,867 average total; $239/hour nationally, $245/hour in California | 21–40 hours per client is most common | You want one person start to finish, at a lower price than a firm |
| Counseling firm (11+ consultants) | $11,033 average total | Varies; ask who is assigned | You need specialists (arts portfolios, recruited athletes, learning differences) under one roof |

Sources: school-counselor time from NACAC and ASCA; independent and firm pricing from the CollegePlannerPro 2025 IEC Pricing Survey. That survey is self-selected, so treat its figures as a market guide, not a price list.

Check the headline number before you act on it. California's widely quoted ratio of 432 students per school counselor includes elementary and middle schools. For high schools specifically, ASCA puts the national range at 195 to 224 students per counselor, which already meets its recommended 250:1. The tighter limit is time: public school counseling departments spend 22% of their time on college admission counseling, versus 51% at private schools.

How we estimated 1.5 hours. Assume about 1,400 working hours a year (roughly 185 school days at 7.5 hours). Twenty-two percent of that is about 310 hours, spread across 195 to 224 students: 1.4 to 1.6 hours per student per year. If every one of those hours went to seniors, who are about a quarter of the caseload, each senior would get about 6. Your school may differ, so ask your counselor how many students they carry and whether your school has a dedicated college counselor.

## The 10 checks

Run these on your first call. A good counselor will welcome every question; hesitation on any of the first four is enough to move on.

### 1. Will they put every fee and the refund policy in writing?

IECA members must disclose fees and financial arrangements in writing before services begin. Refund terms matter as much as price: 72% of surveyed counselors do not refund or transfer unused hours.

- Ask: "Can you send the full fee, what it covers, and your refund policy before we commit?"
- Good answer: A written agreement listing meetings or hours, how many applications and essays are covered, the payment schedule, and what happens if you stop early.
- Verify: Read the agreement for words like "unlimited" or "as needed" with no definition.

### 2. Do they refuse to guarantee results?

IECA members neither guarantee placement nor outcomes, and HECA members do not guarantee admission or financial aid results.

- Ask: "What can you promise us?"
- Good answer: A process, not an outcome: a balanced college list, every deadline met, and applications that represent your student accurately.
- Verify: Search their website and contract for "guaranteed," "money back if not admitted," or promised scholarship amounts.

### 3. Will your student write every word?

IECA members shall not write application essays or any portion of an essay. The stakes are the application itself: the Common App treats another person's language, or the substantive output of an AI tool, submitted as the student's own work as fraud. UC allows advice on content and editing but requires that content and final written text be the student's own, and it may ask applicants to authenticate their writing.

- Ask: "What does your essay help look like, and where do you draw the line?"
- Good answer: Brainstorming, questions and structural feedback; the student drafts and revises every version; no rewritten paragraphs and no AI-generated text.
- Verify: Ask to see an anonymized sample of their feedback. You should see comments and questions in the margin, not replacement sentences.

### 4. Do they take any money from colleges?

NACAC's guide says counselors should not be compensated by commissions or bonuses based on the number of students referred, admitted or enrolled. IECA forbids accepting compensation from schools for placing students and requires written disclosure of any other relationship with a school.

- Ask: "Do you receive any payment, gift or referral fee from a college, program, or vendor you might recommend?"
- Good answer: "No," or a written disclosure you sign, such as "I also consult for this summer program."
- Verify: Ask the same about test-prep and tutoring referrals. Standards differ on referral fees between consultants (HECA allows them if the family is told), but either way you should know.

### 5. Does their membership show up in the association's own directory?

With no state license, association membership is the closest thing to a credential. NACAC has more than 2,000 independent-consultant members, all expected to follow its ethics guide, and IECA members re-pledge to its principles every year.

- Ask: "Which associations are you a member of, and at what level?"
- Good answer: Specific names and membership levels.
- Verify: Search the NACAC directory, IECA and HECA directories yourself. A logo on a website is not proof.
- Keep it in proportion: membership proves a pledge, not skill. Many strong counselors, including former school counselors, belong to none. Treat it as a floor.

### 6. Do they know this year's rules for your student's colleges?

Rules change mid-cycle, and an outdated counselor costs you more than a slow one. The sharpest test for California families right now is UC testing policy.

- Ask: "What is UC's SAT/ACT policy for my student's application year, and what is your plan if it changes?"
- Good answer (as of September 2026): UC does not consider SAT or ACT scores. Whether to bring them back depends on an Academic Senate recommendation due by June 2027, which matters for today's juniors. A good counselor gives you a plan for both outcomes, not a confident prediction.
- Verify: Compare their answer with UC Admissions on the day you ask.

### 7. Do you know who does the work, and how many students they carry?

IECA members must be straightforward about what they are and are not competent to do, and refer out the rest. Caseload tells you how much of that person you get: most solo counselors work with 11 to 20 students per class year.

- Ask: "Will the person on this call be the person who works with my student? How many students are you working with this year? What do you refer out?"
- Good answer: A name, a number, and a clear list of what they don't cover (for example, learning-difference evaluations or recruited-athlete strategy).
- Verify: Put the assigned counselor's name in the written agreement.

### 8. Do they plan for cost and aid, not just admission?

NACAC's guide defines the job as helping students find their best academic, personal and financial college match. An admission your family cannot afford is not a result.

- Ask: "How do you factor our budget and financial aid into the college list?"
- Good answer: They talk about net price calculators, aid deadlines and merit-aid odds before the list is final.
- Verify: Check that the final list includes at least one college you can afford without aid.

### 9. How do they protect your student's information?

NACAC asks counselors to protect student data privacy and not reveal a student's application status or offers without the student's permission. IECA members share confidential information only with written consent.

- Ask: "Where do you store my student's documents, and do you ever share client results publicly?"
- Good answer: A named, access-controlled tool and a consent form before any result or testimonial is published.
- Verify: Look for the consent form in the agreement. No counselor needs your student's application login.

### 10. Do they report results with denominators?

IECA members may claim special competencies only if they are demonstrable, and must not add to admission anxiety. A wall of college logos is neither.

- Ask: "How many students did you work with last year, and how many were admitted to at least one of their top three choices?"
- Good answer: Two numbers, with the counselor explaining what "top choice" meant for their students.
- Verify: Ask for two references from families whose students did not get into their first choice. How the counselor handled that is the real test.

## Six red flags: walk away if you hear these

Any one of these is enough to end the conversation, because each breaks a written standard, not just a preference.

1. "We guarantee admission," or a money-back promise tied to a specific college. Every major association code rules this out (check 2).
2. "I know people in the admissions office." IECA members must avoid even the appearance of influencing admission decisions. A counselor's job is to help your student show why they belong, not to lobby.
3. An offer to write, rewrite, or "polish" essays into their own voice, or to draft with AI. This puts the application itself at risk under Common App and UC rules (check 3).
4. An offer to fill out or submit applications for your student, or a request for their login. The student should own the application from start to finish.
5. Advice to stretch the truth: inflating activity hours, inventing leadership titles, or dramatizing hardship. IECA members may not support work that contains falsified information, and UC screens applications for false information.
6. Pressure to sign today with no written terms. Prepayment by itself is not a red flag; 28% of surveyed counselors require some prepayment. Refusing to put terms in writing is.

## Three extra checks for California families

Most "how to choose a counselor" advice is written for private-college applicants. If your student's list leans on UC and CSU, a counselor also needs to pass these.

1. A–G before GPA. UC requires 15 yearlong A–G courses with a C or better, at least 11 of them finished before senior year, and the courses must be on your school's UC-approved list. A counselor who starts in 10th or 11th grade should audit the transcript against that list in the first meeting. Ask: "Have you checked my student's courses against our school's A–G list?"
2. Personal insight questions without ghostwriting. First-year UC applicants answer four of eight prompts, and UC states that the responses must be independently written by the student in their own writing style. Ask how the counselor coaches PIQs differently from a Common App essay; a good answer covers choosing prompts that show different sides of the student, not polishing one story four times.
3. Testing plans that match UC's current policy. See check 6. A counselor who books SAT prep "for UC" without explaining that UC does not currently consider scores may be selling you hours you don't need. Testing still matters for many private colleges, so the right plan depends on the whole list.

## What it costs, and when you don't need to pay at all

Most independent counselors sell packages, and the price tracks experience more than anything else. In the 2025 survey, average total cost per student was $6,388 nationally and $6,939 in California, and 70% of counselors had raised prices in the past year.

Experience moves the price most: counselors in practice under a year averaged $5,107 per student, versus $7,284 for those with 11 or more years. The firm premium is in the comparison table above. (Same survey: 600 self-selected respondents reporting 2024 prices.)

Two things are worth knowing before you negotiate. Hourly help exists for a narrow job, like a one-time college-list review; the survey's average hourly rate was $239. And 84% of surveyed counselors took on pro bono clients, so it is reasonable to ask about reduced fees.

You may not need a paid counselor if:

- your high school has a dedicated college counselor, not only a general counselor with a mixed caseload;
- your student's list is mostly UC and CSU, their A–G courses are on track, and they are self-directed about deadlines;
- your main need is financial aid forms, which your school, UC and CSU financial aid offices, and free FAFSA and California Dream Act workshops handle well.

A paid counselor earns their fee when your school's college-counseling time is thin, your student's list includes many private or out-of-state colleges with different requirements, or your student needs specialized help (arts portfolios, recruited athletics, learning differences, or first-generation guidance your family can't provide).

## Frequently asked questions

### How much does a private college counselor cost?

In a 2025 survey of 600 independent counselors, the average total cost per student was $6,388 and the average hourly rate was $239. California averages were higher: $6,939 total and $245 an hour. Large firms averaged $11,033.

### When should we start working with a college counselor?

Comprehensive engagements usually run about two years: IECA's member data describes a typical package as an average of 2 years and 30+ hours of service. For California students, an earlier one-time A–G course check in 9th or 10th grade can prevent a problem that no counselor can fix in 12th.

### Is a college counselor worth it?

It depends on how much college-counseling time your school provides and how complex your student's list is. Public school counseling departments spend about 22% of their time on college admission counseling. If your school has a dedicated college counselor and your list is mostly UC and CSU, you may not need to pay.

### What is the difference between a college counselor and an independent educational consultant?

"College counselor" can mean a school employee or a private advisor. NACAC defines an independent educational consultant as a professional working on a fee-for-service basis who serves only students and families, not colleges.

### Can a counselor help with UC personal insight questions?

Yes, within limits. UC encourages applicants to consult counselors and allows advice on content and editing, but the content and final written text must be the student's own.

### Can an AI tool replace a college counselor?

Not for the writing. Yale, for example, says grammar review and early topic suggestions from AI are not fraud, but submitting AI-composed responses can lead to revoked admission. AI tools also carry no professional ethics code or accountability to your family.
