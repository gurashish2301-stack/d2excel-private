D2EXCEL · FREE STUDENT RESOURCES

PAYING FOR COLLEGE · 2026–27 & 2027–28

Over $100 billion in free money for college gets handed out every year. Most of it doesn't come from the scholarship contests you see online.

> **QUICK ANSWER**
>
> The two biggest sources of free money are colleges themselves and the government. Colleges alone give out more than half of all grant money. The government gives out about $46 billion a year, and the Pell Grant reaches 7.5 million students on its own.
>
> Private scholarships — the essay-contest kind you see ads for — only make up a small slice, well under 10% of the total.
>
> The key to unlocking the big money is one form: the FAFSA. If you're starting college in fall 2027, next year's FAFSA opens to everyone by October 1, 2026. A test version is already open now, and submitting during the test period still counts. File as early as you can — a lot of state and school money runs out on a first-come, first-served basis.

## Where the money actually comes from

| Source | How big | Who gets it | How to get it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Money from colleges themselves | About 57% of all grant money — the biggest single source | 22–25% of students at private colleges get merit money; average award is about $12,000/year | You apply to the school. Often awarded automatically based on your application and FAFSA. |
| Government (federal) money | About $46 billion/year. Pell Grant averages $4,500, reaching 7.5 million students | Based on financial need, decided by your FAFSA | Fill out the FAFSA. One form, every year you're in school. |
| State money | About 16% of students get a state award | Varies by state; often first-come, first-served | FAFSA plus your state's deadline (California's is March 2) |
| Private scholarships | About $7–8 billion/year — under 10% of all money given out | About 11–13% of students win one; only about 1.5% win a full ride | You apply to each one separately, one at a time |

## Two things you've probably heard, and how true they really are

Something can be technically true and still not matter much. These two claims shape a lot of student behavior — here's what they actually add up to:

| What you've heard | What's actually true | What it means for you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "There's billions in unclaimed scholarship money out there" | The real number is closer to $100 million — about 0.1% of the $100+ billion given out each year, and most of it has narrow requirements | There's no secret pile of free cash waiting for you. Time spent hunting for "unclaimed money" lists gets you less than almost anything else on this page |
| "Scholarships will pay for my college" | A private scholarship is usually a one-time award worth a few thousand dollars. School and government grants average $15,750–$18,670 a year | Private scholarships are a bonus, not a plan. The plan is FAFSA plus picking colleges that give good aid |

## Important dates (for starting college fall 2027)

| Date | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| Now (Sept 2026) | A test version of next year's FAFSA is open at studentaid.gov/joinbeta and counts as a real submission. Set up your account now (and a parent's). |
| By Oct 1, 2026 | FAFSA opens to everyone, using your family's 2025 tax return. Some private colleges also want the CSS Profile, opening the same day. |
| Oct–Dec 2026 | File your FAFSA around the same time as your college applications — don't wait until after you hear back. |
| Early 2027 | Most state deadlines land here, and that money runs out fast. California's deadline is March 2, 2027. |
| Spring 2027 | Read aid offers carefully. Grants are free; loans get paid back. Some letters mix both — split them apart before comparing schools. |

## Where to spend your time first

1. File your FAFSA early — one form unlocks federal money, most state money, and most college money. Nothing else on this list pays off faster.
2. Pick colleges partly based on their aid, not just their name — every college has to post a "net price calculator" online — use it. Two schools with the same sticker price can end up costing you $20,000+ a year apart once real aid is factored in.
3. Remember that scholarship money is often tied to test scores — even at schools where a test score isn't required to get in, it can still affect how much scholarship money you're offered. That's a real reason to test anyway.
4. For private scholarships, go small and specific — a $1,000 scholarship from a local group with 40 applicants is a much better bet than a $10,000 national one with 40,000 applicants. Look for ones with a small pool of people applying.
