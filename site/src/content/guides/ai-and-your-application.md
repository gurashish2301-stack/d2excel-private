APPLICATIONS & ESSAYS · 2026–27 CYCLE

Most teens use AI for homework. College applications are different, and the rules are written down. Here they are, school by school.

> **QUICK ANSWER**
>
> If AI writes your essay content and you submit it, that counts as fraud under the Common App's rules — which apply at over 1,000 colleges. Yale says it could get your offer taken away, or even get you expelled after you're already enrolled. A few schools, like Brown and Georgetown, don't allow any AI use on the application at all, not even for brainstorming.
>
> At the same time, most schools are fine with — or just haven't said anything about — using AI to organize your thoughts or check grammar, as long as the actual writing is yours. The line is about who's writing the sentences. The moment AI writes any part of what you submit, you've crossed it, everywhere.

## What different schools actually allow

| Type of rule | Schools | What it means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| No AI at all, for anything | Brown (says AI is "not permitted under any circumstances" on the application); Georgetown (bans AI in any part of the application) | Don't even use AI to brainstorm ideas for these schools |
| Writing banned, but early help is okay | Yale — AI-written responses count as fraud (risking your offer or expulsion), but Yale specifically says grammar checks, spelling help, and general topic suggestions are fine | Use AI for ideas and cleanup. Never let it write actual sentences you submit |
| Editing okay, writing not okay | Most schools that have a written policy | AI can point out grammar or clarity issues in something you already wrote. It can't write or rewrite the passage itself |
| No official policy stated | About 71% of the 180 universities studied in a Feb 2026 review | The Common App's fraud rule still applies if that school uses the Common App. No stated policy isn't the same as permission |

## The numbers behind all of this

- A 2025 survey of 200+ admissions officers found — only 2% of colleges officially let students use AI to write their essays. 30% ban it outright. 68% simply have no official policy either way. For using AI just to brainstorm ideas: 27% allow it, 4% don't, and 69% have no stated policy. That's a lot of gray area — which is exactly why checking each specific school's written policy matters.
- A 2026 Pew Research study found — 54% of U.S. teens have used an AI chatbot for schoolwork. Wanting help from AI is completely normal. Where you're allowed to use it on a college application is a different, much stricter question.
- The Common App's fraud policy — specifically calls out using AI-generated writing and calling it your own as fraud. This one policy covers every college that uses the Common App — over 1,000 schools — through a single form you sign when you apply.
- Some schools use software to check for AI writing — but these detectors aren't perfect — they sometimes wrongly flag writing from students who speak English as a second language. Duke stopped giving essays a numerical score partly over concerns about AI. Getting flagged isn't the only risk, either: AI-sounding writing often just reads as generic to the person reading your application, even without a detector involved.

## Green, yellow, and red zones

| Zone | Examples | Rule |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Green — usually fine | Organizing your deadlines; figuring out what a question is actually asking; asking yourself questions to brainstorm about your own life; running spell-check on something you wrote | Still off-limits at the few schools that ban all AI use (Brown, Georgetown) |
| Yellow — depends on the school | Asking for feedback on whether your draft flows well or makes sense; pointing out where your argument feels thin | Check that specific school's policy first. If a school asks whether you used AI, answer honestly |
| Red — fraud, everywhere | Having AI write any sentences you submit; using AI to "polish" descriptions of your activities; copying AI output and changing a few words; running your writing through an AI "humanizer" tool | This breaks the Common App's rules and most individual school policies. The risk doesn't go away even after you're accepted |

## One simple test

Before you submit anything: could you explain every sentence, in your own words, out loud, right now? If yes, it's your writing — no matter what tools helped along the way. If no, it isn't yours, and no rule technicality changes that. Admissions readers aren't looking for perfectly polished writing. They're looking for a real person. That's the one thing AI can't give you — and the one thing you already have unlimited access to.
